PC_BOT: autonomous mobile robot with wireless telemetry

Mobile robot designed from scratch in a team of 5: custom 4-layer PCB in KiCad around an STM32, C firmware for the ToF sensor (I2C) and the nRF24L01+ radio link (SPI), telemetry to a base station.

Goal

PC_BOT is a small wheeled robot designed to map a room. Several robots position themselves along the walls, move autonomously while avoiding obstacles and share their positions over radio. Each robot continuously sends its distances to obstacles, orientation, speed and acceleration to a base station, which builds a 2D map of the room.

Requirements

Function Chosen solution
Autonomous motion 2 DC gear motors driven by PWM through a DRV8411A driver
Obstacle sensing VL53L0X time-of-flight sensor (I2C)
Orientation and motion LSM6DSOX inertial measurement unit (I2C)
Communication nRF24L01+ 2.4 GHz radio module (SPI)
Power 18650 Li-ion battery (3400 mAh), BQ25896 charger over USB-C, 3.3 V regulator
Main controller STM32G431KBT6 microcontroller

Hardware architecture

The sensors (ToF, IMU, charger) share an I2C bus with 4.7 kΩ pull-ups. The radio module sits on a faster, full-duplex SPI bus to stream data continuously. The STM32 is programmed and debugged through an ST-LINK (SWD) connector.

What I did

PCB design (KiCad). Full schematic and layout of a 4-layer SMD board: microcontroller, Li-ion charging chain (BQ25896 with its 1 µH inductor, USB-C connector), 3.3 V regulation, motor driver, sensor and radio connectors, status LEDs and buttons. Each component choice was checked against its datasheet.

C firmware (STM32CubeIDE). Before the final board was ready, each sensor was validated on an STM32 development board:

  • ToF sensor: distance readings over I2C. The sensor detects obstacles from about 3 cm to just under one metre, with a range that depends on ambient light.
  • Radio link: communication between two STM32 boards, each fitted with an nRF24L01+, in transmit and receive mode, with received frames displayed in PuTTY.

Assembly and bring-up. Soldering of the SMD components, then sensor-by-sensor testing on the final board.

Takeaways

PCB design was the longest and most rewarding part: going from a real specification to a manufactured board. The project also taught me that a single layout or soldering mistake can quickly cause a short circuit and damage the board and its sensors, which is why testing each block separately matters.
